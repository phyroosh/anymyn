## 1. Academic & Economic Foundations

1. **The Relative Income Hypothesis (James Duesenberry, 1949):**
   - In *Income, Saving and the Theory of Consumer Behavior*, Duesenberry established that consumption habits are interdependent and sticky.
   - **The Ratchet Effect:** When income increases, individuals quickly raise their consumption standard to align with peers or higher perceived status. However, when income drops, cutting back is psychologically and socially painful. Upward adjustments happen automatically; downward adjustments face severe friction.

2. **Hedonic Adaptation (Brickman & Campbell, 1971):**
   - The psychological tendency for humans to quickly return to a relatively stable baseline of happiness despite major positive events or financial upgrades.
   - Upgrading from a basic phone to a premium flagship, or ordering delivery instead of home cooking, provides an initial dopamine spike, but within weeks becomes the unremarkable "new normal." The individual no longer feels wealthier, yet their monthly burn rate has permanently increased.

3. **Parkinson's Second Law (C. Northcote Parkinson):**
   - *"Expenditure rises to meet income."* Without deliberate, automated guardrails, human spending organically expands to absorb whatever liquid capital is accessible.

4. **Strict Claim Ledger & Grounding:**
   - **Illustrative Model:** The ₹30,000 to ₹40,000 income scenario with ₹3,000 baseline savings is explicitly a hypothetical model designed for educational clarity.
   - **Verified Fact:** Arithmetic integrity (₹40,000 income - ₹37,000 spending = ₹3,000 savings; net savings growth = ₹0.00; savings rate declines from 10.0% to 7.5%).
   - **No Unsupported Statistics:** We do not claim arbitrary percentages such as *"78% of people spend their raises"* or *"9 out of 10 workers are broke."* The narrative focuses strictly on the mathematical mechanics of lifestyle inflation and human behavioral tendencies.

## 2. Audience & Viewer Modeling (The 6 Core Questions)

1. **Who is the intended viewer?**
   - A young professional, student, or salaried worker who has received (or aspires to receive) a pay raise, scrolling through vertical mobile feeds (YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels). Assume zero prior background in economics, accounting, or investing.

2. **What do they already understand about the topic?**
   - They intuitively equate earning more money with becoming wealthier: *"If my boss gives me a raise, I will finally have extra money in the bank."*

3. **What misconception or unanswered question do they hold?**
   - **The Core Misconception:** People assume wealth automatically increases when income increases. They confuse **cash inflow (income)** with **cash retention (wealth/savings)**.
   - **The Unanswered Question:** *"If I make more money now than I did two years ago, why do I still feel stressed at the end of the month?"*

4. **Why would this information matter to them right now?**
   - It directly targets their lived reality: the mysterious disappearance of surplus income. It relieves guilt by explaining the behavioral trap, while offering a simple, actionable rule to prevent it on their very next paycheck.

5. **What specific thing will they understand after watching?**
   - **Single Memorable Takeaway:** A higher salary only makes you richer if you consciously protect the difference. If your spending grows at the exact same speed as your income, you didn't become wealthier—your life just got more expensive.

6. **What would make them immediately swipe away?**
   - Academic jargon (*"marginal propensity to consume"*, *"disposable income elasticities"*).
   - Slow intros, greeting formalities (*"Hey guys, welcome back"*).
   - Preachiness or moral judgment (*"Stop buying lattes, you're irresponsible"*). The script must celebrate that enjoying money is normal, but emphasize conscious allocation.
